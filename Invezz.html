Large-scale employee buyouts are not new, but big tech is changing its meaning

In the early 2000s, mobile phones arrived. Soon, communicating through those clunky, stationary telephones was going out of fashion.

The future was here, and Verizon was not ready.

Customers were abandoning landlines for mobile phones in growing numbers.

The company’s traditional business, once a reliable engine of revenue, was steadily eroding.

Hit by regulatory setbacks amid already difficult conditions, Verizon turned to a familiar corporate instrument: the voluntary buyout.

It offered packages to roughly 152,000 employees.

A few years later, Ford did something similar, extending buyout offers to about 75,000 workers while deep in losses and fighting for survival.

These were two companies in structural trouble, trying to shrink their way toward stability.

For decades, large-scale voluntary separation programs carried a clear meaning. Companies were in distress and needed to cut costs.

That meaning is now changing.

Microsoft's hard decision

In late October 2026, Microsoft sent a different kind of message to a large share of its workforce.

Roughly 60,000 employees, about 27% of the company, received a voluntary exit offer.

It was the largest such program in Microsoft's history.

Sixty thousand is a staggering number. But an even more astonishing figure is $36.6 billion.

That was the company's net income in the three months ended September.

Microsoft reported its results just days after making the buyout offers public. The business was roaring.

Revenue reached $92.3 billion, up 19%. Operating income rose to $45.9 billion.

Net income climbed 32% to $36.6 billion. Azure grew 48%. Wall Street estimates were left behind.

On the same day, Alphabet posted its own strong quarter: revenue of $127.6 billion, up 25%, with Google Cloud more than doubling to $30.7 billion.

The company said it was now running at a half-trillion-dollar annual pace.

And yet, this week, Alphabet also confirmed that it was extending its existing buyout program to five more countries.

The financial profiles of both companies remain robust. And yet, they are increasingly turning to the same tool once reserved for periods of decline.

Meta has also used comparable packages in certain markets.

The approach of large-scale buyouts might not be new, but things are definitely changing.

A different way out

People who study how companies manage their workforces say the shift is revealing.

Wayne Cascio, who has researched downsizing for decades, says such exercises have been around since the 1970s. However, he, too, is seeing a shift.

"Voluntary buyouts started in the 1970s, primarily in manufacturing. During the global financial crisis in 2008, they were widely used by all kinds of companies," Cascio said in a recent Harvard Business Review interview.

"What's changing now is the types of people who are being offered buyouts. In the past, companies usually required employees to have reached a minimum age and minimum tenure — say, people over 55 with at least 10 years at the company.

"Effectively, that means most people were usually within a couple of years of qualifying for retirement. We called them buyouts, but they functioned as early retirement programs. Often, they included early vesting or other retirement sweeteners.

"Microsoft's buyouts have no age limitation and almost no tenure requirement. They're being offered to everyone in certain departments. That approach is becoming more common. Having so many people of all ages affected that's different from what we've seen before."

Why are companies choosing buyouts over outright layoffs?

Cascio explains that voluntary buyouts can give employees a greater sense of agency than abrupt job cuts. That sense of control can also help protect the morale of those who remain.

Josh Bersin, a longtime observer of human resources trends, has described Microsoft's program as something closer to a new management model than a traditional layoff.

"What Microsoft has done is not a layoff, rather a thoughtful workforce innovation, one which many companies will want to copy."

The AI connection

Both Microsoft and Alphabet, like almost all major technology companies, are spending enormous amounts to expand their AI ambitions.

To put money into AI, these companies are having to cut costs somewhere. Reducing employee headcount is one of the ways these giant corporations are doing so.

Bersin argues that this is not necessarily a sign of financial weakness.

"This is not the behavior of a company in distress: it's the behavior of a company thinking ahead."

"By creating space for self-selection out of the organization, they [Microsoft] make room to redeploy talent into AI-critical roles, continuing to hire selectively in high-growth areas, and maintain the engagement of those who stay."

In an era of heavy AI investment, corporate leaders face growing pressure to improve productivity metrics, including revenue per employee.

A measured reduction in headcount, achieved through voluntary means, can support those numbers while companies continue to fund their infrastructure buildout.

Bersin sees the approach as a logical response to the dual demands of technological ambition and financial discipline.

What comes next?

The deeper question is: Where are we headed?

Is this a temporary adjustment, a way for companies to finance enormous AI investments without destroying profitability?

Or is it the early version of a more permanent model, in which large technology firms continuously reshape their workforces through voluntary programs rather than following the old cycle of aggressive hiring followed by abrupt job cuts?

Kristalina Georgieva, managing director of the International Monetary Fund, has warned that AI is "like a tsunami hitting the labor market."

IMF research has found that AI is likely to affect around 60% of jobs in advanced economies and 40% globally.

Dario Amodei, CEO of the IPO-bound Anthropic, has predicted that AI could eliminate half of all white-collar jobs and push unemployment to 20%.

A recent study from Stanford Digital Economy Lab, however, found that AI is not yet causing widespread, economy-wide job displacement, but is aggressively squeezing young workers.

There is a firm belief among many that human judgment will continue to be key.

"The idea that companies will no longer need people is an oversimplification. Human judgment, creativity, customer understanding and accountability remain essential," Bajrang Saharan, CEO of Pressmate OS, told Indian publication India Today.

Even amid gloomy forecasts, the World Economic Forum projects that 170 million new roles will emerge over the same period, resulting in a net gain of 78 million jobs worldwide.

However, WEF also cautions that entry-level jobs remain under immense pressure. It believes that "globally, more than one in three young workers are employed in occupations with medium to high exposure to AI-driven task change."

But one thing is for sure. Change is the only constant, and in the age of AI, that change is happening as rapidly as ever.

What has already changed is the signal these programs send. For much of the past half-century, a large voluntary buyout usually meant a company was in trouble.

How far that redefinition reaches will help determine the texture of work in the technology industry for years to come.
