Horse racing is a team sport; behind every racehorse is a largely unrecognized team of people responsible for the equine athlete’s day-to-day care and exercise. Among those roles is the pony rider, who helps keep the horse and rider calm and safe as they warm up for their race.

Reigning Horse of the Year Sovereignty was escorted to the starting gate for the Breeders’ Cup Classic by pony rider Kaitlin Lutzen. It was the first Breeders’ Cup for both Sovereignty and Lutzen, but they remained calm under pressure, and Sovereignty went home $3.64 million richer.

“It was an amazing experience,” Lutzen said about being called up to pony Sovereignty for the first time that afternoon. “I was smiling ear to ear [in the post parade], just admiring what a great horse he is.”

The Breeders’ Cup Classic was arguably the most important race of Sovereignty’s career. After a sophomore season that saw victories in the Kentucky Derby, Belmont Stakes, and Grade 1 Travers Stakes, he missed the Classic with a fever. In his absence, Forever Young made history as the first Japanese-trained winner of the 1 ¼-mile race.

Sovereignty’s 4-year-old campaign got off to a rocky start with back-to-back defeats in the G2 Oaklawn Handicap and the G1 Stephen Foster, but he was able to get back to the winner’s circle in Saratoga’s G1 Whitney. A showdown against Forever Young in the G1 Jockey Club Gold Cup was not to be when the Bill Mott trainee came down with a foot abscess. He left Saratoga for Keeneland with something left to prove against a competitive field.

All of that meant Lutzen was feeling a little pressure. She gave herself her normal pep talk before taking to the track with Sovereignty in front of a crowd of 45,775: “You got one job: don’t mess up,” she thought.

Lutzen, who also galloped for the Mott barn during the summer, was chosen to pony Sovereignty at the Breeders’ Cup thanks to a special request from jockey Junior Alvarado.

“Junior actually had talked to Bill about it to have me pony instead because they want somebody that can just pick up and go,” Lutzen explained. “You know, not like ease into the gallop, but go because Sovereignty needs a little nitrous boost.

“He finds his pace, and then he settles into the bit, and it’s the nicest warm-up. And we go all the way down. We come back up, and he was just ready. He had a little pep in his step coming out of the tunnel, and somebody snapped a photograph. And I’m just smirking because he knows he’s coming out here to win. He knows what’s going on. He is happy to be here. He’s ready for this race. He is all racehorse and all business.”

After the Classic field was sent on their way, the gate crew got to work moving the starting gate and raking the tire tracks. Lutzen and the rest of the pony riders had their own, much slower race to the gap at the very end of the homestretch.

“We can’t really see the screen, but we can kind of see it,” Lutzen said about their spot at the end of the stretch. “Thankfully, the number two is white. So I just looked for the white blob on the screen, and then as I got closer, I was screaming, ‘Go Sov, go Sov, go Sov.’ As I watched him make his move, I was cheering even louder.

“I could hear Junior telling him thank you as he passed and galloped out,” Lutzen said. “That was big for Junior, and they’ve got a bond. He loves that horse.”

Lutzen was full of praise for the son of Into Mischief.

“His personality is just, it’s classic. He’s classy. He’s kind. He’s always kind, no matter what time of day it is.”
