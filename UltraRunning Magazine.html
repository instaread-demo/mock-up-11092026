The Other Side of Injury

Maggie Guterl08/19/2026
Listen to audio version of this article
0:00 / 0:00 Instaread
Sponsored by

What do you get when you combine ultrarunning, punk rock, skateboarding, tattoos, rainbows, yetis and community? You probably know him, and if you don’t, you should: Jason Green, founder of Yeti Trail Runners. Jason’s name has come up countless times over the year in my conversations with runners. They cite his races as grade “A” examples of what ultrarunning should be about. He asked a very relevant question below while offering his sage punk rock gratitude, but first, let’s get to know him a little better.

Yeti Trail Runners was born in 2011 from casual Tuesday night group runs that grew into much more. Jason’s friends would come over for a run and pizza, and he ended up building trails in his backyard, where he eventually hosted casual running events. The races had names like the Yeti Spaghetti Marathon and the Ultra Beer Mile. Fat-ass-style events (where you just show up and run) were the foundation to a more officially organized event: The Yeti Snakebite 50/50. “We decided to put it on in August in Georgia because it was hot, miserable and seemed like a great time to get a bunch of friends together and do something extremely hard. The idea was that if you survived Snakebite, all the fall races would seem a little easier,” said Jason. Today, Yeti Trail Runners has events across Virgina, Georgia, Illinois, South Carolina and Washington state. You can expect cool swag and skateboard-inspired awards, including custom skateboard decks.

Jason wasn’t always a runner. At 12 years old, he said he “stepped on a skateboard and I’ve never really gotten off one.” He found running later in life in a doctor’s office as he flipped through a magazine and read an article that described running a 50-mile race. He thinks it was likely written by Dean Karnazes (raise your hand if Dean Karnazes’ writing got you into this sport). The article illustrated covering the ultra-distance while cramping and throwing up, and the message of overcoming adversity “spoke to me on the same level skateboarding had spoken to me as a kid,” recalls Jason. Since he was at the doctor’s office, he figured he might as well ask him if he could run 50 miles. The doctor told him it would take years to prepare for a 50-miler. Like any true sane person would, he ignored his doctor’s advice and successfully ran his first 50-miler 11 months later in Mississippi.

Jason took this newfound sport of misfits and dirtbags and moved Yeti Trail Runners from his garage, transforming it into a full-fledged race organization. However, it’s more than just a business, as anyone who has ever run his events could tell you, Yeti Trail Runners is all about community. “Skateboarding taught me a lot about building a community. Most importantly, it taught me there was space for everyone. I took everything I loved about skateboarding and brought it into ultrarunning.” Jason never stops trying to make everyone who comes to a Yeti Trail race feel welcome. He says of his efforts, “The trails belong to everyone. We’ve worked hard to create events where people feel comfortable showing up exactly as they are, whether they’re chasing a podium, finishing their first ultra or simply trying something they never thought possible. The more diverse and inclusive our community becomes, the stronger it gets.”

I leave you with the most punk rock statement from one of the coolest race directors out there. When Jason describes the spirit of Yeti Trail Runners, it’s inclusivity, non-pretentious, welcoming, fun, hard and a little bit quirky. “Even as Yeti has grown, we’ve tried to hold onto that same spirit: no sponsors, no investors, no surrender.”

Jason asked the following:

Did injury and recovery offer crazy perspective in your life? Did you feel like running and being outdoors changed for you on the other side of injury?

I have spoken about this topic before, but it’s an FAQ, so let’s be real. On the conservative side, it is estimated that about 50% of runners will be inflicted by injury each year, and I have been dealing with one for over four years. While I’ve had minor injuries over my 20-year running career, none have been so catastrophic as a 75% Achilles tear and Haglund’s deformity (a bony enlargement on the back of the heel bone that irritates the Achilles tendon and retrocalcaneal bursa causing painful inflammation, swelling and redness where the tendon meets the heel). Surgery was a last resort, and here we are. It doesn’t feel like I’m on the other side yet, but none-the-less, I am hopeful. That being said, it has offered a crazy perspective and anything we do in life that is hard has got to make us a better person, right?

Not Perishing: The funniest thing I’ve noticed is that people will tell you they wouldn’t be able to survive an injury with time off from running. I am here to tell you that you will survive. I am not stronger or more mentally stable – I am like you, except on the other side (or heading through to the other side) of an injury, and never wanted to put myself in the shoes of someone who was injured before I got injured. It was just too terrible to imagine. But we do what we need to do when presented with adversity. Isn’t that why we chose the sport of ultrarunning in the first place? We’re here to see what challenges arise and how we can solve them. I found ways to cope over four years of dealing with this injury; you deny, get proactive, find patience and eventually, hopefully, find a solution, even if it doesn’t make the injury go away forever.

Gratitude: In early September last year, less than two weeks before I was due to have Achilles surgery, I remember trudging up the last climb on the last day of a massive three-day hut trip in the Italian Alps, watching my friend Stephanie Case become a tiny dot 2,000 feet above me (or 600 meters for our non-US friends). I was in pain, and I felt slow and bad about myself for not being able to keep up with my friend. I kept reminding myself to remember this moment while I was at home on the couch recovering from surgery. So, I relished in the beauty, daunting climbs and surrounding vistas of the Italian Alps. Now, seven months post-surgery, when I get outside for shorter runs or hikes, I have a greater appreciation for my surroundings, what my body can do and the potential for what I might be able to do again.

I get asked often what races I have signed up for or what ultras I hope to do in the future and the truth is, I don’t know. My one true goal is to be strong and healed enough to head out with my friends and enjoy a long day (or days) in the mountains. Maybe I won’t ever run a race competitively again, or at all, but if I can enjoy several days pain-free in the mountains away from technology and the normal buzz of modern life, I will be a satisfied and grateful human. And even if there is never another pain-free day in my running journey, at least my feet look good now (nah, probably not ever).

Community: The truth is you survive these things with community. Your running circle of friends may dwindle. Some friends are just running friends and that’s OK. You see some friends less because your common bond was running. But in these times, you find out which of your running friends are real friends. There are countless theories and principles on how many close friends you have in life, but this column is already 500 words past the limit. The running friends will be there and can pick back up right where you left off, if they are OK going a little slower. In the meantime, the real ones will ask you to game night, brunch or to drive an hour to the gym to work out together.

You don’t have to be passive in this arrangement, and you can still show up to running in a different way. Go to races and volunteer or crew. You’ll still see your running friends and the community will make it all worth it. We got into ultrarunning to challenge ourselves and see what we can overcome, but we stay because of the people.

My goal, as running shifts, is to keep that same spirit the Yeti Trail Runners was built on. Be yourself, show up for others, shred the gnar and keep a positive mental attitude (PMA). Stay punk!
